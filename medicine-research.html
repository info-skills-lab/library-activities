Combine your keywords with AND and OR

Searching a database is easy, but different to using Google. To search a database effectively you need to combine your keywords using AND and OR.

From here, the examples use a second topic: low carb diets, cardiovascular disease and risk factors.

What is OR used for?

OR is used to combine similar keywords together. Use it to capture the different ways a concept is described in an article.

Use "cardiovascular disease" OR "heart disease" to find articles that contain the word cardiovascular disease as well as articles that contain the word heart disease.

What is AND used for?

Once you have your synonyms grouped together, the final step in building your search is to combine the concepts using AND.

In our example we have three concepts:

  1. "cardiovascular disease*" OR "heart disease*"
    AND
  2. "low carb* diet*" OR "carbohydrate restricted diet*"
    AND
  3. "risk factor*"

Your final search results will now include articles relevant to your topic as each article will include all three key concepts.

Asterisk *

( * ) is used to capture different word endings, e.g. low carb* diet* will capture low carb diet, low carb diets, low carbohydrate diet as well as low carbohydrate diets.

"Quotation marks"

(" ") are used when searching a phrase, e.g. "risk factor" will capture these two words next to each other.

Database limits

Databases allow you to refine your search results, e.g. to date published, peer reviewed, publication type etc.

Search health check

Searching databases can be a process of trial and error. Your first search may give you too many results, so you will need to refine it to make it more relevant for your topic.

Open the infographic in a new window

Text version

Too many results?

  • Choose appropriate terms. Ensure your terms are not too broad or narrow.
  • Try phrase searching. A search in quotation marks will look for the terms side by side.
  • Refine to resource type. You can often refine your search to articles, eBooks, etc.
  • Use the right database. Check the UNSW Library Subject Guides for databases relevant to your topic.

Not enough results?

  • Avoid unnecessary terms. General terms like impact, characteristics or influence may not appear in the articles you need.
  • Check your spelling. Databases often search exactly what you type, including errors.
  • Try alternative terms. Check the titles, abstracts and subject terms of relevant articles for alternative search terms. Look at the references and citations as well.

Evaluating sources

For a therapy question, the type of study matters. The NHMRC evidence hierarchy ranks study designs for intervention questions.

NHMRC evidence hierarchy - intervention

  1. Level IA systematic review of level II studies
  2. Level IIA randomised controlled trial
  3. Level III-1A pseudorandomised controlled trial
  4. Level III-2A comparative study with concurrent controls
  5. Level III-3A comparative study without concurrent controls
  6. Level IVCase series with either post-test or pre-test/post-test outcomes

Source: NHMRC Evidence Hierarchy, intervention questions, as reproduced by Cancer Australia (Appendix 1).
